## 1. What it is

**Classic ML & data science** is the pre-deep-learning core that still wins a huge
fraction of real problems: **tabular machine learning** (gradient boosting, linear
models, trees), **feature engineering**, **classical statistics** (estimation,
hypothesis testing, confidence intervals), and **experiment design** — especially
**A/B testing** — built on the **scikit-learn / pandas / statsmodels** stack.

It exists as a named skill because **not every problem needs an LLM (or even a
neural net).** For structured/tabular data — churn, fraud, credit risk, demand
forecasting, ranking, conversion — a gradient-boosted tree on good features
routinely *beats* deep learning, trains in seconds, costs nothing to serve, and is
interpretable. And every product decision (including "did this AI feature help?")
is settled by *statistics and A/B tests*, not vibes. Knowing when the simpler tool
wins is senior judgment.

## 2. Why employers want it

Section 11 ranks this #31 and notes it's *still asked at big-tech ML roles* (S8,
S13, S15, R7), with the rationale that *"knowing when a simpler model wins is
senior judgment."* The signal:

- **You pick the right tool.** You don't reach for a transformer when XGBoost on
  tabular data is faster, cheaper, better, and explainable.
- **You can measure impact rigorously.** A/B testing, significance, power, and
  pitfalls (peeking, multiple comparisons) — the math that turns "it feels better"
  into a defensible decision (ties to #17).
- **You understand the fundamentals.** Bias–variance, regularization,
  cross-validation, leakage, calibration — concepts that *also* govern LLM
  evaluation and fine-tuning.
- **You handle real data.** Feature engineering, missing values, imbalance, and
  the messy tabular reality most businesses actually run on.

## 3. Mental model

**Two pillars: predict well from structured data, and decide truthfully from
experiments.**

```
  PILLAR 1 — TABULAR PREDICTION
    data → feature engineering → model → validate honestly → deploy
    bias–variance: underfit (high bias) ◄──► overfit (high variance)
       regularization + cross-validation find the sweet spot.
    gradient boosting = fit many shallow trees SEQUENTIALLY, each correcting the
       previous one's residual errors → strong, robust default for tables.

  PILLAR 2 — STATISTICAL DECISION-MAKING
    hypothesis: H0 (no effect) vs H1 (effect)
    run experiment → estimate effect + uncertainty → p-value / confidence interval
    A/B test: randomize users to A vs B, compare a metric, ask
       "is the difference bigger than noise?" (and is it big enough to matter?)
    guard against: peeking, multiple comparisons, underpowered tests, novelty.
```

**Concepts to own cold:**

- **Bias–variance & generalization.** Train ≠ test performance; the gap is
  overfitting. Cross-validation estimates real performance; regularization
  (L1/L2, tree depth/leaves, early stopping) controls it.
- **Data leakage** — the #1 silent killer: using information at training time that
  won't exist at prediction time (target leakage, or fitting a scaler on the full
  dataset before splitting). Inflated offline metrics, real-world collapse.
- **The right metric.** Accuracy lies under class imbalance; use precision/recall/
  F1, ROC-AUC (ranking quality), PR-AUC (imbalanced), log-loss/Brier
  (calibration). Match the metric to the cost structure.
- **Gradient boosting** (XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost) — the tabular champion:
  sequential trees on residuals/gradients, handles mixed types, missing values,
  and nonlinearity with little preprocessing. Usually the first thing to try on a
  table.
- **Statistical testing** — t-test/z-test (means), chi-square (proportions),
  Mann-Whitney (non-parametric); **p-value** (P(data this extreme | H0)),
  **confidence interval** (range of plausible effects), **power** (P(detect a real
  effect)) — you compute the needed sample size *before* running.
- **A/B testing pitfalls** — peeking (checking early inflates false positives →
  use sequential tests or fixed horizons), multiple comparisons (more metrics →
  more false wins → correct, e.g. Bonferroni/FDR), Simpson's paradox, novelty/
  primacy effects, SRM (sample-ratio mismatch).
- **Calibration & interpretability** — do predicted probabilities mean what they
  say? SHAP/feature importance explain *why* — often required in regulated domains
  (#30).

## 4. The landscape

**Tabular modeling**
- **scikit-learn** — the workhorse: linear/logistic regression, SVM, random
  forests, gradient boosting, pipelines, cross-validation, metrics, preprocessing.
  → [`../01.ml/`](../01.ml/).
- **Gradient boosting:** **XGBoost** (the Kaggle standard), **LightGBM** (fast,
  large data, leaf-wise), **CatBoost** (great with categorical features). The
  go-to for tabular accuracy.
- **Newer tabular DL:** TabPFN (foundation model for small tables), FT-Transformer,
  TabNet — closing the gap but GBMs still usually win on real business tables.

**Data wrangling & features**
- **pandas** / **Polars** (fast), **NumPy**, **feature-engine**, **category_encoders**,
  **featuretools** (automated features), `sklearn.preprocessing`/`ColumnTransformer`.

**Statistics & experimentation**
- **statsmodels** (regression, tests, time series), **scipy.stats** (distributions,
  tests), **pingouin** (friendly stats), **Bayesian** (PyMC, `bambi`).
- A/B platforms: **Statsig**, **Optimizely**, **GrowthBook**, **Eppo**; or roll your
  own with proper power analysis.

**Time series & forecasting:** statsmodels (ARIMA), **Prophet**, **sktime**,
**Darts**, gradient boosting on lag features.

**Interpretability:** **SHAP**, LIME, permutation importance, partial dependence.

**Visualization / EDA:** matplotlib, seaborn, plotly, `ydata-profiling`.

**Tradeoffs:** linear/logistic models = interpretable, fast, strong baselines, but
limited nonlinearity. Trees/RF = nonlinear, low-tuning, but can overfit. **GBMs =
best tabular accuracy**, more tuning, less interpretable (use SHAP). Deep learning
on tabular = rarely worth it unless huge data or mixed modalities. For *deciding*,
frequentist A/B is standard; Bayesian gives intuitive "P(B better)" and handles
early stopping more gracefully. The senior move is matching tool to problem — and
often the answer is "a GBM and a clean A/B test," not an LLM.

## 5. Worked example

The two canonical artifacts: a **gradient-boosting baseline with honest
cross-validation** and an **A/B test analyzed correctly** (effect, significance,
*and* practical magnitude). Runs on CPU in seconds with synthetic data.

```python
"""classic_ml.py — a tabular GBM baseline + a correctly-analyzed A/B test.
Run: pip install scikit-learn scipy ; python classic_ml.py
"""
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# --- 1. Tabular prediction: a GBM with HONEST cross-validation (no leakage) ---
X, y = make_classification(n_samples=2000, n_features=20, n_informative=8,
                           weights=[0.8, 0.2], random_state=0)  # imbalanced
gbm = GradientBoostingClassifier(n_estimators=100, max_depth=3, random_state=0)
# Use ROC-AUC, not accuracy — accuracy is misleading at 80/20 imbalance.
scores = cross_val_score(gbm, X, y, cv=5, scoring="roc_auc")
print(f"GBM 5-fold ROC-AUC: {scores.mean():.3f} ± {scores.std():.3f}")
print("  (cross-val gives an HONEST estimate; a single train score would overfit)")

# --- 2. A/B test: did variant B lift conversion over A? ---
# Simulate: A converts at 10.0%, B at 11.5% — is the lift real or noise?
n_a, n_b = 8000, 8000
conv_a = rng.binomial(n_a, 0.100)
conv_b = rng.binomial(n_b, 0.115)
p_a, p_b = conv_a / n_a, conv_b / n_b

# Two-proportion z-test (H0: p_a == p_b).
p_pool = (conv_a + conv_b) / (n_a + n_b)
se = np.sqrt(p_pool * (1 - p_pool) * (1 / n_a + 1 / n_b))
z = (p_b - p_a) / se
p_value = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z)))

# 95% CI for the lift (the part people forget — significance ≠ magnitude).
se_diff = np.sqrt(p_a * (1 - p_a) / n_a + p_b * (1 - p_b) / n_b)
lift = p_b - p_a
ci = (lift - 1.96 * se_diff, lift + 1.96 * se_diff)

print(f"\nA/B test: A={p_a:.3%}  B={p_b:.3%}  lift={lift:+.3%}")
print(f"  z={z:.2f}  p={p_value:.4f}  95% CI for lift=({ci[0]:+.3%}, {ci[1]:+.3%})")
decision = "SHIP B" if p_value < 0.05 and ci[0] > 0 else "INCONCLUSIVE — keep testing"
print(f"  decision: {decision}")
print("  (require BOTH significance AND a CI that excludes 0 / is worth the effort)")
```

The lesson is the discipline: a GBM is the right *first* model for a table and
cross-validation gives an honest score (a single train-set number would lie); and
an A/B test is only actionable when you report the **effect, the significance, AND
the confidence interval** — a tiny "significant" lift on a huge sample may not be
worth shipping. This is the statistical rigor behind every #17 ship decision.

## 6. How to learn it

**Beginner (weeks).** Do EDA with pandas; train logistic regression and a random
forest with scikit-learn; learn the train/validation/test split, cross-validation,
and why accuracy misleads under imbalance. Compute a t-test and a confidence
interval by hand. *Milestone:* a clean notebook: load → EDA → model → honest
evaluation, with no leakage.

**Job-ready (1–3 months).** Win (or place well on) a tabular task with **XGBoost/
LightGBM** + feature engineering + proper CV + hyperparameter search; explain it
with SHAP; design and analyze an A/B test end-to-end including **power analysis**
(sample size *before* running) and the peeking/multiple-comparisons pitfalls.
*Milestone:* a tabular model with a defensible eval *and* an A/B test you can
analyze and defend.

**Expert.** Diagnose subtle leakage and distribution shift; calibrate
probabilities; design experiments under constraints (CUPED variance reduction,
sequential testing, interference/network effects); choose frequentist vs Bayesian
appropriately; and *know when not to use ML at all*. *Milestone:* you own modeling
*and* experimentation decisions a business bets money on.

## 7. Interview signals

What's probed: fundamentals, honest evaluation, and statistical rigor — plus the
judgment of *when* classic beats deep/LLM.

**Q: "Tabular churn prediction — what model and why?"**
Strong answer: start with a **gradient-boosted tree** (XGBoost/LightGBM) — strong
on tabular data, handles mixed types/missing values/nonlinearity with little
preprocessing, trains fast, and is explainable via SHAP. Establish a logistic-
regression baseline first. Reach for deep learning only with huge data or mixed
modalities. Evaluate with cross-validation and a metric matched to the cost (ROC/
PR-AUC under imbalance), watching for leakage.

**Q: "Your A/B test shows p=0.04. Ship it?"**
Strong answer: not automatically. Check: was the test pre-registered with a fixed
horizon (no peeking)? Is it adequately powered? Does the **confidence interval**
exclude zero *and* clear a practical-significance threshold? Any SRM or novelty
effect? Multiple metrics tested (correct for it)? Significance without magnitude or
with a flawed design isn't a ship signal.

**Q: "What is data leakage and how do you prevent it?"**
Strong answer: leakage is using information at train time that won't exist at
prediction time (target leakage, or fitting preprocessing on the full data before
splitting) — it inflates offline metrics and collapses in production. Prevent it by
splitting *first*, fitting all transforms inside a pipeline on the train fold only,
and checking features for future information.

**Q: "When would you NOT use an LLM?"**
Strong answer: for structured/tabular problems (fraud, risk, forecasting, ranking)
a GBM is faster, cheaper, more accurate, and interpretable; for a decision question
you need an A/B test and statistics, not generation. Using the simplest sufficient
tool is the senior call.
