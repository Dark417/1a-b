## 1. What it is

**Domain expertise** is deep, working knowledge of a *vertical* — healthcare,
finance, legal, defense, life sciences, manufacturing — and/or the meta-skill of
**rapidly acquiring** enough of a new domain to build the right thing in it. It's
the understanding of a field's workflows, vocabulary, data, constraints,
regulations, failure costs, and what "good" actually means to its experts.

It exists as a named hiring skill because **the hard part of applied AI is the
domain, not the model.** A foundation model is a commodity API; the differentiated
value is knowing *which* clinical decision actually needs support, *what* a
radiologist's workflow tolerates, *which* legal errors are catastrophic, and
*what* data a bank is even allowed to use. Forward-Deployed Engineers (FDEs) live
at exactly this seam — sitting with a customer, learning their world, and turning
fuzzy domain needs into a working system.

## 2. Why employers want it

Section 11 ranks this #30 and flags it **FDE-weighted**: valued at Anthropic
Applied-AI (S3, with a life-sciences variant) and Palantir/Salesforce FDE (S19,
S20). The rationale: *"the hard part of applied AI is the domain, not the model —
earns trust and unlocks real use cases."* The signal:

- **You build the *right* thing.** You understand the user's actual workflow and
  the cost of each failure mode, so you scope features that matter (ties to #17).
- **You earn expert trust.** Speaking the vocabulary and respecting the
  constraints (clinical, regulatory, financial) is what gets domain experts to
  adopt your tool.
- **You navigate constraints.** HIPAA, SOC 2, GDPR, FDA, model-risk management,
  audit trails — you design for them from day one, not as an afterthought.
- **You context-switch.** FDEs jump between customers/verticals; the ability to
  *learn a domain fast* is itself the skill.

## 3. Mental model

**Domain expertise sits *between* the user's world and the model.** Your job is to
translate one into the other faithfully — and the translation is lossy unless you
understand both sides.

```
   DOMAIN WORLD                          AI SYSTEM
   ────────────                          ─────────
   workflows & jobs-to-be-done   ◄──►   features / agent design
   vocabulary & ontology          ◄──►   prompts / schemas / eval rubrics
   data (messy, regulated, siloed)◄──►   pipelines / RAG corpus / labels
   constraints (legal, safety)    ◄──►   guardrails / HITL / audit logs
   "what good means" to experts   ◄──►   evaluation criteria & acceptance bar
                  ▲
                  └─ the FDE/applied engineer translates BOTH directions
```

**The acquisition playbook (how to learn a domain fast):**

- **Shadow the expert.** Watch the real workflow before designing anything. The
  documented process and the actual process differ — the gap is where AI helps or
  hurts.
- **Learn the ontology.** Every domain has a controlled vocabulary and
  relationships (ICD/SNOMED in medicine, GAAP in finance, citation formats in
  law). Get the terms right or lose credibility instantly.
- **Map the failure cost asymmetry.** A wrong movie recommendation is cheap; a
  missed tumor, a bad trade, a hallucinated legal citation, or a misclassified
  target is catastrophic. The acceptable error profile (and thus the
  HITL/guardrail design) is domain-specific.
- **Find the regulatory perimeter.** What data can you touch? What must be logged,
  explained, retained? Who's liable? Design within it from the start.
- **Define "good" with experts.** The eval rubric must encode *their* standard, not
  yours — which means co-building the gold set and the acceptance bar with domain
  experts (ties to #07).

**The recurring AI-in-vertical patterns:** document intelligence (legal/healthcare/
finance are document-heavy → RAG + extraction), decision support (assist, don't
replace, the expert → HITL), compliance/audit (everything traceable → #22),
and proprietary-data grounding (the moat is the domain corpus → RAG over
fine-tuning, usually).

## 4. The landscape

**High-value verticals and their AI shape**
- **Healthcare / life sciences** — clinical notes, imaging, literature; ontologies
  (ICD-10, SNOMED, UMLS); constraints: HIPAA, FDA (SaMD), high failure cost →
  decision support + HITL. Anthropic/others run life-sciences applied teams.
- **Finance / fintech** — fraud, risk, compliance, research, trading; constraints:
  SOC 2, model-risk management (SR 11-7), explainability/audit, low latency.
- **Legal** — contract review, e-discovery, research; *hallucinated citations are
  career-ending* → grounding + verification mandatory; privilege/confidentiality.
- **Defense / government** — intelligence, logistics; constraints: clearances,
  air-gapped/on-prem, provenance, accountability (Palantir's domain).
- **Manufacturing / industrial** — predictive maintenance, quality, ops (GE
  Vernova S30); sensor/time-series data, safety-critical.
- **Insurance, retail, energy, education** — each with its own data, workflows, and
  rules.

**Constraint frameworks to know:** HIPAA, GDPR, CCPA, SOC 2, ISO 27001, FedRAMP,
the EU AI Act risk tiers, sector model-risk rules. Data residency, PII handling
(→ #19), and audit/explainability requirements drive architecture.

**The role that embodies this — the FDE:** Forward-Deployed Engineer (Palantir's
coinage, now Anthropic/OpenAI/Sierra/many startups). Sits with the customer,
learns the domain, builds and iterates *in situ*. Distinct from a research or
platform engineer: success = the customer's outcome, in their language.

**Tradeoffs:** deep single-vertical expertise (a moat, but narrow) vs broad
context-switching range (FDE flexibility, but shallower per domain). The strongest
profile pairs *one* deep vertical with a repeatable *method* for acquiring new
ones. Beware: domain knowledge without engineering can't ship; engineering without
domain builds the wrong thing — the value is the *combination*.

## 5. Worked example

The concrete artifact of domain expertise is a **domain-aware requirements + eval
spec** — capturing the workflow, the failure-cost asymmetry, the regulatory
perimeter, and an expert-defined acceptance bar *before* a line of model code.
Here it is as runnable, inspectable structure (a legal contract-review feature).

```python
"""domain_spec.py — encode domain knowledge into a buildable, gateable spec.
Run: python domain_spec.py
"""
from dataclasses import dataclass, field


@dataclass
class DomainSpec:
    vertical: str
    job_to_be_done: str                 # the EXPERT's actual task, in their words
    ontology_terms: list[str]           # vocabulary you MUST get right
    failure_costs: dict[str, str]       # error type -> consequence (asymmetry!)
    regulatory: list[str]               # the perimeter you must design within
    human_in_loop: bool                 # does an expert review before action?
    acceptance_bar: dict[str, float]    # "good" as the expert defines it

    def is_buildable(self) -> tuple[bool, list[str]]:
        """A domain feature is buildable only when the non-model parts are pinned."""
        gaps = []
        if not self.regulatory:
            gaps.append("regulatory perimeter undefined — legal blocker")
        if not self.acceptance_bar:
            gaps.append("no expert-defined acceptance bar — can't gate ship")
        # High-cost domains MUST keep a human in the loop until trust is earned.
        if any("catastrophic" in c.lower() for c in self.failure_costs.values()) \
           and not self.human_in_loop:
            gaps.append("catastrophic failure cost but no human-in-the-loop")
        return (len(gaps) == 0, gaps)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    spec = DomainSpec(
        vertical="Legal — contract review",
        job_to_be_done="Flag risky clauses for a lawyer to review (NOT auto-advise).",
        ontology_terms=["indemnification", "limitation of liability",
                        "governing law", "termination for convenience"],
        failure_costs={
            "hallucinated citation": "catastrophic — sanctions, lost license",
            "missed risky clause": "high — client liability",
            "false flag": "low — extra lawyer review time",
        },
        regulatory=["attorney-client privilege", "confidentiality", "no legal advice"],
        human_in_loop=True,                 # lawyer reviews every flag
        acceptance_bar={"recall_risky_clauses": 0.95, "citation_grounding": 1.00},
    )
    ok, gaps = spec.is_buildable()
    print(f"{spec.vertical}: buildable={ok}")
    print("  failure-cost asymmetry drives design:",
          [k for k, v in spec.failure_costs.items() if "catastrophic" in v])
    print("  acceptance bar (expert-defined):", spec.acceptance_bar)
    print("  gaps:", gaps or "none — domain context pinned, safe to build")
```

The lesson: domain expertise is *executable judgment*. The spec encodes what only
someone who understands legal practice knows — that hallucinated citations are
catastrophic (so grounding must be 100% and a lawyer must review), that "missed
risky clause" recall is the metric that matters, and that the tool must *flag, not
advise*. An engineer without the domain would have built an auto-advisor and gotten
someone disbarred.

## 6. How to learn it

**Beginner (weeks).** Pick one vertical (ideally one you have prior exposure to).
Learn its core vocabulary, top 3 workflows, and primary regulations. Interview or
shadow one practitioner. *Milestone:* you can describe a real workflow and its
failure-cost asymmetry in the domain's own terms.

**Job-ready (1–3 months).** Build a real AI feature *for* the domain with a
domain-expert in the loop: co-define the eval rubric and acceptance bar, design
within the regulatory perimeter, and ship behind HITL. Practice the FDE loop —
turn a fuzzy domain ask into a scoped spec (the example above) in an afternoon.
*Milestone:* a deployed domain feature an actual expert trusts and uses.

**Expert.** Develop deep credibility in one vertical *and* a repeatable method for
ramping into new ones (the acquisition playbook); navigate procurement, compliance,
and change management; translate fluently in both directions so experts and
engineers align. *Milestone:* domain experts treat you as a peer, and you can
parachute into an unfamiliar vertical and ship within weeks.

## 7. Interview signals

What's probed (esp. FDE/applied loops): can you understand a domain and build the
*right* thing trustfully within its constraints?

**Q: "A hospital wants AI to help triage patients. How do you approach it?"**
Strong answer: start with the workflow and the *failure-cost asymmetry* (missing a
critical case is catastrophic; a false alarm is cheap-ish but causes alarm
fatigue), so design **decision support with a clinician in the loop**, not
automation. Pin the regulatory perimeter (HIPAA, possibly FDA if it's a medical
device), co-define "good" with clinicians (recall on critical cases), ground in the
hospital's data, and log everything. Shows you lead with domain, not model.

**Q: "How do you ramp into a domain you don't know?"**
Strong answer: shadow practitioners, learn the ontology/vocabulary, map the top
workflows and the cost of each failure mode, find the regulatory perimeter, and
co-define the acceptance bar with experts — *before* building. The meta-skill is a
repeatable acquisition method, not pre-existing knowledge.

**Q: "Why do so many AI pilots fail to reach production in regulated industries?"**
Strong answer: the demo ignored the domain — wrong failure-cost model, no HITL
where trust isn't earned, a regulatory/data blocker discovered late, or an eval bar
that wasn't the experts' bar. The fix is leading with domain context: scope to the
real workflow, design within constraints, and earn trust incrementally.

**Q: "Tell me about a time you translated a fuzzy business need into a system."**
Strong answer: the FDE loop — clarified the *job*, learned the domain constraints,
scoped an MVP with explicit non-goals, co-built the eval with stakeholders, shipped
behind HITL, and iterated from real usage. Concrete metric movement is the proof.
