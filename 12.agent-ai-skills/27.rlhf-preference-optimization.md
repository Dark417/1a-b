## 1. What it is

**RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)** and **preference
optimization** are the family of techniques that turn a raw, pretrained language
model into a *helpful, harmless, honest* assistant by training it on **human
preferences** ("response A is better than response B") rather than on a fixed
target. It spans **reward modeling** (learn what humans prefer), **policy
optimization** (PPO, GRPO), the **direct** methods that skip the reward model
(DPO and its variants), the **data pipelines** that produce preference pairs, and
the newer **RL-for-reasoning** line (DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI o1) that uses verifiable
rewards to teach models to *think*.

It exists because next-token pretraining produces a model that can *continue* text
but doesn't reliably *do what you ask*, refuse harm, or follow instructions. There
is no labeled "correct answer" for "write a helpful, safe reply" — but humans can
*compare* two answers. Preference optimization converts those comparisons into a
training signal. It is the step that made ChatGPT/Claude usable.

## 3. Mental model

**The three-stage post-training pipeline (classic RLHF):**

```
  1. SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning)
     teach the format: fine-tune on (prompt, good-response) demonstrations.
     model now follows instructions, but isn't yet preference-tuned.

  2. REWARD MODEL (RM)
     collect pairs: prompt → (chosen, rejected) ranked by humans.
     train a model r_θ(prompt, response) → scalar, with the Bradley-Terry loss:
        L = -log σ( r(chosen) - r(rejected) )
     i.e. push the chosen response's reward above the rejected one's.

  3. RL (PPO)
     optimize the policy π to MAXIMIZE reward, with a KL penalty to a frozen
     reference so it doesn't drift into gibberish that games the RM:
        objective:  E[ r(x, y) ]  -  β · KL( π(y|x) || π_ref(y|x) )
     PPO = clipped policy-gradient updates; needs RM + policy + ref + value model
     (4 models in memory → heavy, finicky).
```

**The DPO insight (why it took over).** DPO proves you don't need a separate
reward model or RL loop. The optimal RLHF policy has a *closed form* in terms of
the reference policy and the reward; substituting that into the Bradley-Terry loss
gives a **simple classification loss on preference pairs** you optimize with plain
backprop:

```
  L_DPO = -log σ( β·log[π(chosen)/π_ref(chosen)] - β·log[π(rejected)/π_ref(rejected)] )
```

In words: increase the policy's relative log-prob of the *chosen* response and
decrease it for the *rejected*, anchored to the reference by β. No RM, no PPO, no
value model — just two models (policy + frozen ref) and a stable loss. This is why
most open fine-tunes use DPO.

**RL-for-reasoning (the R1/o1 line).** For tasks with a *checkable* answer (math,
code), you don't need human preferences — you use a **verifiable reward** (did the
answer pass the test?) and RL (PPO/**GRPO**). **GRPO** (DeepSeek) drops the value
model: sample a *group* of responses per prompt, use their mean reward as the
baseline, and push above-average ones up. This is how models learn long
chain-of-thought reasoning. → ties to evaluation (#07) and reasoning.

**Variants to know:** **IPO** (fixes DPO overfitting), **KTO** (learns from
*unpaired* good/bad labels — cheaper data), **ORPO** (combines SFT + preference in
one stage, no reference model), **RLAIF / Constitutional AI** (AI feedback instead
of human, scales oversight → #19), **rejection sampling / Best-of-N** (sample many,
keep the RM's favorite — simple and strong).

## 4. The landscape

**Algorithms (by data + compute cost)**
- **SFT** — required first stage; demonstrations.
- **PPO** — classic RLHF; most powerful, most complex (4 models, unstable, reward
  hacking risk). Still used at labs.
- **DPO** — the practical default; offline, stable, 2 models. Variants: **IPO**,
  **KTO** (unpaired), **ORPO** (no ref, fused with SFT), **sDPO**, **SimPO**.
- **GRPO / RLOO / Reinforce++** — group/leave-one-out baselines, lighter than PPO;
  GRPO is the RL-for-reasoning workhorse (verifiable rewards).
- **Rejection sampling / Best-of-N / RAFT** — sample, score with RM, fine-tune on
  the best; simple and effective.
- **RLAIF / Constitutional AI** — AI feedback guided by written principles.

**Tooling**
- **TRL** (HF) — `RewardTrainer`, `DPOTrainer`, `PPOTrainer`, `GRPOTrainer`,
  `KTOTrainer`, `ORPOTrainer`. The practical toolkit. →
  [`../09.huggingface/trl`](../09.huggingface/trl).
- **OpenRLHF**, **veRL** (volcengine), **trlX**, **Axolotl** (config-driven SFT/
  DPO), **Unsloth** (fast), **NeMo-Aligner** (NVIDIA), **AllenAI open-instruct**.

**Data**
- Preference datasets: **UltraFeedback**, **HH-RLHF** (Anthropic helpful/harmless),
  **Nectar**, **SHP**, **HelpSteer** (NVIDIA). Pipelines: human annotation
  (Scale/Surge), LLM-as-judge labeling (RLAIF), and on-policy sampling.

**Reward models:** scalar Bradley-Terry RMs, **process reward models** (score each
reasoning step), **generative RMs** (an LLM judges and explains), and
**verifiers** for code/math.

**Tradeoffs:** PPO = highest ceiling, hardest to run, reward-hacking prone. DPO =
90% of the value at a fraction of the complexity (start here). GRPO = for
verifiable-reward reasoning tasks. The bottleneck is almost always **data quality**
(noisy preferences poison everything) and **reward hacking** (the model games the
proxy) — which is why the KL anchor and good eval matter.

## 5. Worked example

The heart of DPO made explicit: the **DPO loss** as a few lines of PyTorch, showing
exactly how chosen/rejected log-probs and the reference policy combine. Runs on CPU
with synthetic log-probs so the *math* is the lesson.

```python
"""dpo_loss.py — the DPO objective from first principles (no RM, no RL loop).
Run: python dpo_loss.py
"""
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F


def dpo_loss(policy_chosen_logp, policy_rejected_logp,
             ref_chosen_logp, ref_rejected_logp, beta: float = 0.1):
    """All inputs are summed log-probs of the response under each model.
    Intuition: raise policy's relative log-prob of CHOSEN, lower it for REJECTED,
    measured AGAINST the frozen reference (the log-ratios), scaled by beta."""
    # log[ π(y)/π_ref(y) ]  for chosen and rejected
    chosen_logratio = policy_chosen_logp - ref_chosen_logp
    rejected_logratio = policy_rejected_logp - ref_rejected_logp
    # The "implicit reward margin": how much more the policy prefers chosen vs rejected
    logits = beta * (chosen_logratio - rejected_logratio)
    # Bradley-Terry: maximize P(chosen > rejected) = minimize -log σ(margin)
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(logits).mean()
    # bookkeeping: implicit rewards (used to track reward-margin / accuracy)
    chosen_reward = (beta * chosen_logratio).detach()
    rejected_reward = (beta * rejected_logratio).detach()
    return loss, chosen_reward, rejected_reward


if __name__ == "__main__":
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    # Synthetic per-example summed log-probs (negative; closer to 0 = more likely).
    # Case A: policy already prefers chosen over rejected MORE than the ref does → low loss.
    pc, pr = torch.tensor([-2.0]), torch.tensor([-5.0])   # policy: chosen >> rejected
    rc, rr = torch.tensor([-3.0]), torch.tensor([-3.5])   # ref: roughly equal
    loss, cr, rj = dpo_loss(pc, pr, rc, rr)
    print(f"good case  loss={loss.item():.4f}  reward margin={cr.item()-rj.item():+.3f}")

    # Case B: policy prefers the REJECTED one → high loss, training pushes it down.
    pc2, pr2 = torch.tensor([-5.0]), torch.tensor([-2.0])
    loss2, cr2, rj2 = dpo_loss(pc2, pr2, rc, rr)
    print(f"bad case   loss={loss2.item():.4f}  reward margin={cr2.item()-rj2.item():+.3f}")
    assert loss2 > loss, "preferring the rejected response must cost more"
    print("\nDPO turns 'A is better than B' into a stable classification loss —")
    print("no reward model, no PPO, just policy vs frozen reference.")
```

The whole point: a preference pair becomes a *log-sigmoid of a reward margin*,
where the "reward" is the policy-vs-reference log-ratio. Training nudges chosen
responses up and rejected down, with β controlling how far the policy may drift
from the reference. TRL's `DPOTrainer` is this loss plus the machinery to compute
those log-probs on real tokenized batches.

## 6. How to learn it

**Beginner (weeks).** Understand the three-stage pipeline (SFT → RM → RL) and *why*
each exists. Read the InstructGPT and DPO papers. Train a tiny reward model on a
preference dataset and inspect its rankings. *Milestone:* you can explain RLHF and
DPO at a whiteboard and name what each stage fixes.

**Job-ready (1–3 months).** SFT then **DPO** a small open model with TRL on a real
preference set (UltraFeedback/HH-RLHF); evaluate before/after on a benchmark and on
refusals; try a variant (KTO/ORPO). Build a small preference-data pipeline
(on-policy sampling + LLM-as-judge labeling). *Milestone:* a DPO-tuned model that
measurably improves helpfulness/safety with a written eval.

**Expert.** Run full PPO and GRPO (RL-with-verifiable-rewards for math/code);
diagnose reward hacking and KL collapse; build process/generative reward models;
design RLAIF/Constitutional pipelines; reason about on- vs off-policy data and
distribution shift. *Milestone:* you train a reasoning or alignment improvement
end-to-end and can defend the data + reward design.

## 7. Interview signals

What's probed: conceptual depth on alignment and awareness of the current frontier.

**Q: "Explain the RLHF pipeline."**
Strong answer: SFT on demonstrations (format/instruction-following) → train a
reward model on human preference pairs with a Bradley-Terry loss → optimize the
policy with PPO to maximize reward *under a KL penalty* to a frozen reference (so
it doesn't game the RM into gibberish). Note the 4-model cost and reward-hacking
risk.

**Q: "Why did DPO largely replace PPO?"**
Strong answer: DPO derives that the optimal RLHF policy has a closed form, so you
can skip the separate reward model and the RL loop and optimize a simple
classification loss on preference pairs directly — only policy + frozen reference,
stable, much cheaper. PPO still has a higher ceiling and on-policy benefits, but
DPO gives most of the value far more practically.

**Q: "How are reasoning models like R1/o1 trained?"**
Strong answer: RL with **verifiable rewards** — for math/code you can *check* the
answer, so you reward correctness (and format) and use PPO/**GRPO** to reinforce
chains of thought that lead to right answers. GRPO drops the value model by using
the mean reward of a *group* of sampled responses as the baseline. This teaches
long deliberate reasoning without human preference labels.

**Q: "What is reward hacking and how do you mitigate it?"**
Strong answer: the policy exploits flaws in the reward model to get high reward
without truly being better (verbosity, sycophancy, format tricks). Mitigations: the
KL penalty to the reference, better/diverse preference data, reward-model
ensembles, regularization, and *evaluating the policy*, not just its reward.
