## 1. What it is

**Research literacy** is the ability to *consume and produce* the primary
literature of AI: read a paper critically, extract the real contribution from the
hype, **reproduce** its results from the description, build on it, and — for
research-track roles — **publish** your own work at peer-reviewed venues (NeurIPS,
ICML, ICLR, ACL, EMNLP, CVPR). It includes tracking the firehose of new work
(arXiv, conferences, the model-release cadence) and separating signal from noise.

It exists as a named skill because **AI moves monthly**, not yearly. The technique
your product depends on may be three weeks old and described only in a paper.
Someone on the team must be able to read it, judge whether it's real, reproduce it,
and ship it — *before* it's in a library. And for labs, peer-reviewed publication
is the credible, external evidence that you can do original work.

## 2. Why employers want it

Section 11 ranks this #28 and flags it **research-track**: required at Meta/DeepMind
research (S6, S7, S12), a strong signal at labs even when not strictly required.
The signal:

- **You stay current without hand-holding.** You can read the paper that defines a
  new method and apply it before anyone writes the blog post.
- **You can reproduce.** Turning a paper's terse description + an unmaintained repo
  into working, verified results is a hard, valuable skill (most "results" don't
  reproduce on the first try).
- **You evaluate claims critically.** You spot weak baselines, cherry-picked
  benchmarks, missing ablations, and overclaiming — so the team doesn't chase
  hype.
- **Publications are credentials.** A first-author paper at a top venue is
  hard-to-fake evidence of research ability; it gates research-scientist roles.

## 3. Mental model

**Reading a paper is triage, not linear reading.** Use a layered pass (Keshav's
"three-pass" method):

```
  PASS 1 (5 min): title, abstract, figures, conclusion → What problem? What claim?
                  Is this relevant? (most papers stop here.)
  PASS 2 (1 hr):  method + experiments, skim math, read all figures/tables.
                  → How does it work? Are the baselines fair? What's the ablation?
  PASS 3 (deep):  reproduce the math/derivation, re-implement, re-run.
                  → Do I believe it? Could I have done it? What breaks?
```

**The critical-reading checklist (what separates literacy from skimming):**

- **What is the actual contribution?** New method, new dataset, new analysis, or
  just scale? Strip the framing.
- **Is the baseline strong and fair?** The most common flaw is beating a *weak* or
  *untuned* baseline. A 2% gain over a crippled baseline is nothing.
- **Ablations.** Which component actually causes the improvement? No ablation = no
  understanding.
- **Reproducibility.** Code? Seeds? Hyperparameters? Compute? Can a reader rebuild
  it?
- **Statistical significance.** One run or several? Error bars? On LLMs, eval
  variance is large.
- **Limitations & failure modes.** A good paper names them; a hype paper hides
  them.

**The reproduction reality:** papers under-specify (a missing learning-rate
schedule, a data-cleaning step, an eval prompt) and code rots. Reproduction is
detective work: match the environment, find the undocumented knobs, and reconcile
your numbers with theirs — *and report honestly when they don't match.*

**Producing research (the loop):** question → literature review (what's known) →
hypothesis → minimal experiment → ablate → write (motivation, method, fair
experiments, honest limitations) → peer review → iterate. **Reviewers reward
clarity, fair comparison, and ablations** over raw novelty.

## 4. The landscape

**Finding & tracking work**
- **arXiv** (cs.CL, cs.LG, cs.CV), **Papers with Code** (paper↔repo↔leaderboard),
  **Semantic Scholar** / **Google Scholar** (citation graph, "cited by"),
  **Connected Papers** (visual neighborhood), **alphaXiv** (commentary).
- Curation: **Hugging Face Papers** (daily), **arxiv-sanity**, newsletters (The
  Batch, Import AI, AlphaSignal), X/Bluesky researchers, lab blogs (Anthropic,
  DeepMind, OpenAI, Meta AI, Google Research).

**Venues (the credibility ladder)**
- ML: **NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR**. NLP: **ACL, EMNLP, NAACL**. Vision: **CVPR, ICCV,
  ECCV**. Workshops at these venues are a lower-stakes entry; **arXiv preprints**
  are how work actually spreads first.

**Reproduction & experiment tooling**
- **Weights & Biases** / **MLflow** (experiment tracking, the lab notebook),
  **Hydra** (config sweeps), **Papers with Code** repos, Docker (env capture),
  seeded runs, **ML Reproducibility Challenge**.

**Reading & writing tools**
- **Zotero** (reference manager), **Overleaf**/LaTeX (writing), **NotebookLM**/LLM
  assistants for first-pass summaries (verify everything — they hallucinate
  citations), **Elicit**/**Consensus** (literature search).

**Tradeoffs / pitfalls:** don't outsource *understanding* to an LLM summary — it
flattens nuance and invents references; reproduce before you trust a benchmark;
prefer primary sources (the paper) over secondary (a thread); beware
leaderboard-chasing (overfit benchmarks ≠ real progress). Reading breadth (triage
many) and depth (reproduce a few) are both needed.

## 5. Worked example

The single highest-leverage research-literacy artifact: a **structured paper
review** template, plus a tiny reproduction-tracking harness that forces you to
verify a claim rather than trust it. The template is the deliverable; the code
makes "did it reproduce?" a checked fact.

```python
"""paper_review.py — a structured critical-review template + reproduction check.
Run: python paper_review.py
"""
from dataclasses import dataclass, field


@dataclass
class PaperReview:
    title: str
    venue_year: str
    # --- the questions that separate literacy from skimming ---
    problem: str                       # what gap does it address?
    contribution: str                  # the ACTUAL novel claim (strip the hype)
    method_one_line: str               # core mechanism in one sentence
    baselines: list[str]               # are they strong + fairly tuned?
    has_ablation: bool                 # is the cause of the gain isolated?
    reproducible: str                  # "code+seeds" | "code only" | "none"
    headline_result: tuple             # (metric, their_number)
    limitations: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    my_verdict: str = ""

    def credibility_flags(self) -> list[str]:
        flags = []
        if not self.has_ablation:
            flags.append("no ablation — can't attribute the improvement")
        if self.reproducible == "none":
            flags.append("no code/seeds — reproducibility unverifiable")
        if len(self.baselines) < 2:
            flags.append("thin baselines — gain may be vs a weak comparison")
        if not self.limitations:
            flags.append("no stated limitations — possible overclaiming")
        return flags


def reproduction_check(their_number: float, my_number: float, tol: float = 0.01) -> str:
    """Honest reporting: reproduced, close-but-off, or failed."""
    diff = abs(their_number - my_number)
    if diff <= tol:
        return f"REPRODUCED (Δ={diff:.3f} within tol)"
    if diff <= 3 * tol:
        return f"PARTIAL (Δ={diff:.3f}) — chase the undocumented knob"
    return f"FAILED to reproduce (Δ={diff:.3f}) — report it honestly"


if __name__ == "__main__":
    r = PaperReview(
        title="Direct Preference Optimization",
        venue_year="NeurIPS 2023",
        problem="RLHF (PPO) is complex/unstable; need a simpler alignment method.",
        contribution="Closed-form optimal policy → a simple classification loss; no RM/RL.",
        method_one_line="Optimize log-ratio of chosen vs rejected against a frozen ref.",
        baselines=["PPO-RLHF", "SFT", "Best-of-N"],
        has_ablation=True,
        reproducible="code+seeds",
        headline_result=("win-rate vs SFT", 0.68),
        limitations=["offline; sensitive to preference-data quality"],
    )
    print(f"REVIEW: {r.title} ({r.venue_year})")
    print("  contribution:", r.contribution)
    flags = r.credibility_flags()
    print("  credibility flags:", flags or "none — looks sound")
    # Suppose I re-ran and got 0.675 win-rate:
    print("  reproduction:", reproduction_check(r.headline_result[1], 0.675))
```

The lesson: research literacy is *structured skepticism*. The template forces the
questions that catch weak papers (missing ablations, thin baselines, no
reproducibility), and the reproduction check makes "it works" a verified number
with honest reporting when it doesn't — exactly the discipline labs hire for.

## 6. How to learn it

**Beginner (weeks).** Read foundational papers with the three-pass method
("Attention Is All You Need," GPT/BERT, a recent SOTA model report). Write a
one-paragraph summary + critique of each (problem, contribution, weakness). Follow
HF Papers / Papers with Code daily. *Milestone:* a running paper-notes log where
each entry states the contribution *and* a critique.

**Job-ready (1–3 months).** **Reproduce** a paper end-to-end from its description +
repo; reconcile your numbers with theirs and write up what you changed. Track
experiments with W&B. Participate in a paper-reading group; review a paper as if
for a conference. Pick a subfield and read its last 12 months. *Milestone:* a
public reproduction (blog/repo) of a non-trivial paper with verified or honestly
reported results.

**Expert.** Run the full research loop: a novel question, strong baselines,
ablations, and a clear write-up; submit to a workshop then a main conference;
review for venues. Track SOTA well enough to predict what's next and de-risk the
team's bets. *Milestone:* a first-author paper (or a widely-used reproduction/
open model) and a track record of calling real advances early.

## 7. Interview signals

What's probed (research roles): can you read, reproduce, critique, and produce?

**Q: "Walk me through a recent paper you found important and your critique of it."**
Strong answer: state the problem and the *actual* contribution crisply, explain
the method in a sentence, then critique fairly — were the baselines strong? was
there an ablation isolating the cause? did it reproduce? what are the limitations?
Showing you can *praise and puncture* the same paper is the signal.

**Q: "This paper claims SOTA. How do you decide whether to believe it?"**
Strong answer: check the baselines (strong and tuned?), look for ablations, see if
results are over multiple seeds with error bars, check whether code/hyperparams are
released, and ideally reproduce the headline number. Beware leaderboard overfitting
and weak-baseline gains.

**Q: "How do you stay current?"**
Strong answer: a triage pipeline — daily skim (HF Papers/arXiv/Papers with Code),
deeper read of the few that matter, a reading group, and *reproducing* the ones I'd
build on. Primary sources over threads; verify LLM summaries.

**Q (research roles): "Describe a project from question to result."**
Strong answer: the loop — gap in the literature → hypothesis → minimal experiment →
ablation → honest limitations → write-up/peer review. Emphasize fair comparison and
what you'd do if it *didn't* work.
