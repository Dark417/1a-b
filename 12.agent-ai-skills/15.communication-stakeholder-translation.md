# 15 · Communication & Stakeholder Translation

> Skill #15 (Tier 2 — Important). *Explaining AI clearly and turning needs into
> plans.* ~50%, weighted up by seniority and FDE roles — required at DeepMind,
> Anthropic, and every forward-deployed role. See the rationale in
> [`../11.agent-ai-engineer/03.ranked-skills.md`](../11.agent-ai-engineer/03.ranked-skills.md).

---

## 1. What it is

The ability to **explain technical AI work to non-experts and leadership**, and
to **translate fuzzy customer/business needs into a concrete architecture and
plan** — plus clear writing (docs, design proposals, postmortems, updates). It's
the human-facing half of AI engineering.

It exists because **AI work is cross-functional and ambiguous, and the bottleneck
is often alignment, not code.** A correct system built to solve the wrong problem
is worthless; a brilliant result no one understands doesn't get adopted.

## 2. Why employers want it

Explicitly required at DeepMind (`S6`), Anthropic (`S3`), and every FDE role
(`S2`, `S4`, `S19`, `S20`); it scales with seniority. The signal:

- **You de-risk projects** by surfacing assumptions and aligning stakeholders
  *before* building.
- **You make AI legible** — explaining capabilities, limits, risks, and tradeoffs
  honestly to people who decide and fund.
- **You turn ambiguity into a plan** — the core of forward-deployed and senior
  work: scope, architecture, milestones, risks.

## 3. Mental model

**Communication = audience-adaptive compression + translation.** You hold the
full technical picture and render the *right slice* for the listener.

```
                      ┌──────────────── you ────────────────┐
business need ──────► │ translate: need → requirements → plan │ ──► aligned plan
(fuzzy, in their      │ explain:   results/risks/tradeoffs    │ ──► informed decision
 language)            │            at the audience's altitude │
                      └──────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Translate up (need → plan):** clarify the *real* goal (not the stated one), the
constraints (latency, cost, accuracy, data), and the definition of success;
convert into requirements, an architecture, milestones, and named risks. Manage
expectations about what AI *can't* reliably do.

**Translate down/across (results → understanding):** lead with the **so-what**
(the decision/impact), then support; match the **altitude** to the audience (exec:
impact/cost/risk; PM: capability/tradeoffs/timeline; engineer: mechanism); use
analogy and concrete examples; be honest about uncertainty and limitations.

**Principles that always hold:** *BLUF* (bottom line up front), one idea per
paragraph, concrete > abstract, show don't just tell (demo/example), name
assumptions and risks explicitly, quantify when you can, and **calibrate
confidence** ("this works ~80% of the time; here's the failure mode") rather than
over-promising.

**For AI specifically:** explain non-determinism ("it won't be 100% right"),
hallucination risk, the cost/latency/quality triangle, and why evaluation matters
— in plain language a stakeholder can act on.

## 4. The landscape

**Modes of communication.**

| Mode | Audience | Keys |
|---|---|---|
| Exec update / brief | leadership | BLUF, impact/cost/risk, one slide, no jargon |
| Technical design doc | engineers | problem, options + tradeoffs, decision, risks |
| Customer discovery | client/FDE | listen, clarify the real need, define success |
| Demo | mixed | concrete, narrated, honest about limits |
| Postmortem | team | blameless, timeline, root cause, actions |
| Async written update | all | status, blockers, asks, next steps |

**Requirements translation (the FDE/senior core).** Discovery questions ("what
does done look like?", "what's the cost of an error?", "what data do we have?") →
written requirements → architecture proposal → phased plan with milestones and
risks → expectation-setting on AI's limits.

**Writing craft.** Structure (BLUF, headers, one idea per paragraph), the
inverted pyramid, plain language, concrete examples, and editing for concision.
Visuals: a simple architecture diagram and a single decision-driving chart beat
paragraphs.

**Stakeholder management.** Identify who decides/funds/uses; tailor the message;
manage expectations early; surface tradeoffs as *choices* ("faster vs cheaper vs
more accurate — pick two") rather than dictating.

**Frameworks.** Pyramid Principle (Minto), BLUF, the "explain it to a smart
non-expert" test, and pre-mortems (imagine it failed — why?) for risk surfacing.

## 5. Worked example

Translating a vague request into an aligned plan, and the same result explained
at three altitudes — the two everyday acts of this skill. (No code; this *is* the
deliverable.)

**The request (what a stakeholder says):**
> "Can we build an AI that answers customer questions from our help docs?"

**The translation (discovery → requirements → plan):**

```
1. Clarify the real need (discovery questions):
   - Which questions? (top 20 support tickets vs the long tail)
   - What's the cost of a wrong answer? (annoyance vs financial/legal)
   - Latency target? Volume? Languages? Who reviews failures?
   - What docs/data exist, and how fresh must answers be?

2. Requirements (written, agreed):
   - Ground answers in our docs with citations (no hallucinated policy).
   - ≥90% helpful on the top-50 questions; escalate to a human when unsure.
   - p95 latency < 3s; English first; doc index refreshed daily.

3. Architecture (one diagram): RAG over the help docs + citations + an
   "I don't know → escalate" path + an eval set on the top-50 questions.

4. Plan & expectations (phased, with risks):
   - Phase 1: RAG MVP on top-50 + eval harness (2 wks).
   - Phase 2: hybrid retrieval + reranking + guardrails (2 wks).
   - Risk: docs are stale/contradictory -> fix data first (skill #14).
   - Honest limit: it will not be 100% correct; we measure, escalate, improve.
```

**The same result, three altitudes:**

- **To an exec:** "The assistant resolves ~90% of common support questions with
  cited answers, escalating the rest to humans. It cuts response time from hours
  to seconds and should reduce ticket load ~30% — at roughly $X/month, with a
  measured quality bar we monitor."
- **To a PM:** "It's RAG over the help docs: it retrieves the relevant passages
  and answers from them with citations, and says 'I don't know' (escalates) when
  retrieval is weak. It's strong on the top-50 questions, weaker on the long
  tail; we gate every change on an eval set."
- **To an engineer:** "Hybrid retrieval (BM25 + dense) over chunked docs, a
  reranker, citation-forcing prompt, a confidence threshold for escalation, and
  Ragas faithfulness/context-recall in CI. Lost-in-the-middle mitigated by
  reranking the top-k."

Notice the *content* is constant; the **altitude, jargon, and so-what** change
per audience — and the plan leads with the *real* goal and names the risks and
honest limits. That calibration is the whole skill.

## 6. How to learn it

**Beginner (0–3 months).** Practice BLUF; write a one-paragraph "what I built and
why it matters" for every project; explain a technical concept to a non-technical
friend. *Milestone:* a project README an exec could read and understand the value
of.

**Intermediate / job-ready (3–9 months).** Write design docs with options +
tradeoffs; run a discovery conversation and produce written requirements; give a
demo that's honest about limits; present at the right altitude to two different
audiences. *Milestone:* you turned a vague ask into an agreed plan and shipped
against it.

**Expert (9+ months).** Lead stakeholder alignment on ambiguous projects;
de-risk via pre-mortems; manage expectations on AI's limits with leadership;
mentor others' communication. *Milestone:* you're trusted to own the
client/leadership relationship on a project, not just the code.

## 7. Interview signals

- **"Explain RAG (or transformers) to a non-technical executive."** They're
  testing audience-adaptation: analogy, so-what, no jargon, honest about limits —
  not a lecture.
- **"A stakeholder asks for X that AI can't reliably do. What do you say?"**
  Clarify the underlying goal, explain the limit honestly, propose what *is*
  achievable, and reframe the tradeoff — not "yes" or a flat "no."
- **"Walk me through how you'd scope a fuzzy AI request."** Discovery questions →
  requirements → architecture → phased plan with risks and success criteria →
  expectation-setting.
- **Behavioral: "Tell me about a time you explained something technical / aligned
  stakeholders / handled a disagreement."** Structured, specific (STAR), and
  showing you led with the listener's needs.
- **Watch-for in any answer:** do they lead with the bottom line? Calibrate
  confidence? Name assumptions and limits? That *is* the signal.
